Quantum computing outlook

Current quantum computers are not yet reliable or large enough to outperform strong classical methods on realistic day-ahead plant scheduling. If hardware and algorithm trends continue, useful hybrid quantum methods could emerge in roughly 6–7 years. This is a scenario to test, not a forecast: we would compare solution quality, runtime, and cost with the classical baseline as the technology improves.

Quantum hardware report

We have local warm-start experiments, but no verified physical-QPU results or matched end-to-end runtime comparison. The graphs below distinguish measured HiGHS behavior from the evidence still needed for a hardware claim.

What we are testing

The existing 24-hour experiments cover 5, 10, 20, and 40 plants. A coarse QUBO proposes plant commitments over time blocks; repair makes a candidate feasible, then HiGHS solves the full-day MILP using that candidate as a start. The 5- and 10-plant cases use locally simulated QAOA. The 20- and 40-plant cases use simulated annealing because their 80- and 160-bit QUBOs are too large for the current local QAOA setup. No QAOA angle tuning or sampling in these experiments ran on a QPU.

The question is whether a feasible starting schedule helps HiGHS find good solutions earlier or reduces its solve time on a harder instance. The cases measured so far all close at the root node, leaving little search tree to improve.

Results we have: HiGHS stage only

These are the published medians from QUANTUM_WARM_START.md, measured on a shared qBraid instance with one HiGHS thread. Each case used three solver runs, except 40 plants, which used two. Cold and warm runs both finished at one branch-and-bound node. The warm-start preparation, local sampling, and repair times are excluded from the solve-time graph.

The warm start lowered the reported primal-dual integral in all four cases. HiGHS solve times were 0.202 → 0.195 s, 0.198 → 0.187 s, 0.607 → 0.505 s, and 3.081 → 3.021 s from 5 to 40 plants. The repaired starting schedules were 1.12%–2.19% above the optimum; HiGHS still reached the optimum in both modes.

End-to-end runtime: awaiting matched trials

A fair comparison needs total wall-clock time for the same valid cases, including QAOA or annealing preparation, tuning, sampling, repair, and HiGHS. A QPU run would also include queue and transfer time. The current measurements do not include those components, so they cannot establish an end-to-end speedup.

To populate this comparison, collect repeated cold and complete hybrid timings on identical inputs, validate both final schedules with the same checker and cost model, and record machine, software versions, seeds, shots, and backend. The chart generator accepts those trials in speed_results.csv.

Actual QPU results

No physical-QPU job or measured bitstring counts are recorded for this project. The existing output.csv labels its 24-hour schedule as simulator and has no job ID, so it cannot populate a hardware histogram. A QPU would measure candidate commitment bitstrings; repair and MILP would still run classically.

To populate this histogram, save the device and job IDs for one verified physical-QPU run, its full bitstring counts and total shots, and the circuit's depth and two-qubit gate count. Then calculate the valid-shot share and compare those counts with an ideal or noisy simulator for the same circuit. The chart generator accepts these counts in hardware_results.csv.

What the evidence suggests

The local pipeline produced feasible warm starts close to optimal and improved the primal-dual integral, but it has not shown a material total-runtime advantage. All four measured MILPs solved at the root node; a harder case with a real search tree is needed to test whether the start can reduce search. Physical hardware also needs a smaller, shallower circuit and a measured QPU run before any hardware-performance conclusion is possible.
